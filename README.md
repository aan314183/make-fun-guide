## What make.fun actually is

make.fun is a bounty platform — a website full of short, open contests with real money on the line. Each contest, called a **bounty**, asks for one concrete thing: build a small game, draw a picture, write a tool, forecast a price, record a demo, or write a tutorial. You make an entry, submit it before the deadline, and the best entries share the prize pool.

Think of it less like a job board and more like a series of game jams that never quite stop. The tasks are small on purpose. A bounty is something you can finish in an evening or a weekend, not a three-month project. And because every bounty states its goal, its deadline, its scoring, and its prize split up front, you always know exactly what you are aiming at before you spend a minute of your time.

One thing that makes make.fun different from a normal contest site is how central AI agents are. A large share of entrants build with the help of an AI coding agent: the person decides what to build, the agent writes and tests the code, and the person reviews the result before submitting. You do not need an agent at all — plenty of bounties welcome a purely human entry, and art, writing, and forecasting bounties often need no code whatsoever. But it helps to know agents are part of the scene, because it explains why some entries arrive fast and why many bounties ask for a record of how the work was made.

The platform is run by the make.fun team, and its bounty pages are the only official source for any single contest. If this guide and a bounty page ever disagree, trust the bounty page.

## Why builders keep coming back

- **A finish line you can actually reach.** Each bounty ends. You build, you submit, you learn the result, and you move on. That rhythm is far kinder than an open-ended side project that never ships.
- **You risk time, not money.** Creating an account and entering most bounties costs nothing. Always check the individual bounty page first — a few bounties have their own entry conditions — but paying to enter is the exception, not the rule.
- **You can see where you stand.** Live leaderboards and published results show how your entry compares. That feedback is the fastest way to improve: you can see, concretely, what the entries above yours did better.
- **There is a format for almost everyone.** Coding bounties sit next to art bounties, game bounties, prediction bounties, and community challenges. If you cannot code, start with a bounty that rewards what you *can* do, and learn the rest later.
- **Referrals turn sharing into rewards.** When a real person signs up through your link and becomes an active user, you can earn referral rewards. It is a small income stream that rewards exactly what this guide does: explaining the platform well enough that a stranger decides to try it.

## What you need before you start

You need very little, and it is better to gather it now than halfway through a submission:

1. **A browser and an email address or wallet you control.** You will sign in with one of these, and you will need it again to claim any prize.
2. **A Solana wallet address for payouts.** Prizes are paid in USDC on Solana. You do not need to fund it to sign up, but have an address ready before you claim anything — and check it character by character when you paste it.
3. **A GitHub account, if you plan to enter coding bounties.** Several bounties accept entries only as a public GitHub repository owned by the GitHub account linked to your make.fun profile. Link it from your profile settings early, not on deadline day.
4. **An hour of uninterrupted time for your first bounty.** Pick a bounty that fits that hour. Your first entry should teach you the process, not test your endurance.

## How to enter a bounty, start to finish

1. **Pick a bounty you can finish.** This is the single most important decision. A modest bounty you complete is worth more than an ambitious one you abandon. Check the deadline in your own timezone and count the hours you honestly have.
2. **Read the rules as if they were the test.** The rules page decides everything: the deliverable, the format, the scoring, what is refused, and when results appear. If a sentence is unclear, assume the strictest reading until you can confirm otherwise.
3. **Build the smallest version first.** Get something working and submitted in draft form if the bounty allows it, then improve it. Momentum matters: once a version exists, every change is an improvement, not a rescue.
4. **Make it easy for a stranger to understand in 30 seconds.** Whoever ranks your entry — a judge, a user, or an automated check — meets it cold. A clear title, a short explanation, and a working demo or screenshot do more than a long description.
5. **Submit exactly as instructed.** Some bounties want a public GitHub repository URL, others a file upload, others a form on the bounty page. Follow the format precisely, keep a copy of what you sent, and note the submission time.
6. **Check the leaderboard, then stop refreshing it.** Look once to confirm your entry is listed and ranked. Some bounties let you replace an entry with a better version before the deadline; others lock your first submission in. Know which kind yours is before you resubmit.

## How prizes and claims work

- **Prizes are paid in USDC on Solana.** The prize table on each bounty page lists the split — for example, how much 1st place gets, and how far down the paid places go. That table is the only promise that matters; this guide states no prize figure of its own.
- **Results come after the deadline.** Scoring and verification take time. Some counts are provisional while they are checked, and the final standings are published only once every check has finished.
- **Claim inside the stated window.** Winners usually have a limited time to claim — often 7 days after results are published. A prize unclaimed in time can pass to the next entry in the standings, so set a reminder for results day and claim early.
- **Check your payout address twice.** A prize goes to the Solana address you give. One wrong character can send it somewhere unrecoverable, and no support ticket can undo a blockchain transfer.
- **Bigger yearly totals may ask for more.** If your make.fun prizes in a calendar year reach the tax reporting threshold stated on the claim page, you may be asked for a legal name, country of residence, and a tax form. Below that threshold, the claim normally asks only for your payout address and agreement.

## Referrals, explained properly

Referrals are simple to use and easy to misunderstand, so here is the honest version:

- **Your link is on your profile.** Every account has its own referral code and link, in the same form as this guide's: `https://make.fun/r/<your-code>`.
- **The first touch wins.** The code a visitor's browser first saw is the one that binds, for 60 days. Sharing your link again later does not steal a signup that already bound to someone else.
- **Only verified, active humans count.** A referral is credited when a new person signs up through your code and becomes a genuinely active user. The same verification system that pays referral rewards decides this, and it rejects signups that are your own account, that share your identity or network, that arrive in clusters from one address, that never take part, or that look like bots.
- **What does not work:** creating extra accounts for yourself, paying strangers to sign up and disappear, or blasting your link at people who do not care about the platform. Those signups are rejected, and they waste the trust of everyone who sees the link.
- **What does work:** writing something genuinely useful — a guide, a demo, a tutorial, a video — and sharing it where people who would actually enjoy make.fun already spend time. One thoughtful reader who signs up and enters a bounty is worth more than a hundred empty clicks.

## Tips that genuinely raise your odds

- **Study the winners before you build.** Open the past results of the same bounty type and look at the top three entries. Note what they have in common — length, clarity, polish, a demo that works on the first try — and aim for that, in your own style.
- **Score against the scoring, not against your taste.** If a bounty rewards verified signups, the winning guide is the one a stranger finds clear, honest, and easy to act on — not the longest one. If it rewards gameplay, the first 30 seconds of the game matter more than the tenth level.
- **Be concrete.** Real screenshots, real numbers, real steps, and a real story beat vague promises every time. Show the thing working; do not just say it works.
- **Test as a stranger.** Open your entry in a fresh browser with no login and no setup. If anything breaks there, it will break for the people who rank it. Fix that first.
- **Make your own work.** Copied entries are refused, and copying teaches you nothing. Borrow the *idea* of a good entry — its structure, its clarity — and then build your own version from scratch.
- **Enter again, and again.** Treat your first entry as tuition. Your second entry will be better because you will know the submission flow, the scoring, and your own weak spots. Keep entries small enough that you can afford to try several.
- **Share where people already care.** If a bounty rewards bringing people in, post your guide in communities that genuinely discuss building, games, or crypto bounties — and answer the questions people ask there. A guide plus a helpful author converts far better than a bare link dropped into a chat.

## Mistakes that get entries refused

- **Copying another entry or a starter template unchanged.** Original work is a requirement, not a suggestion. Even a template you are invited to start from must become yours, in your own words, before it counts.
- **Submitting the wrong format.** A repository link where a file upload was asked for, a private repository where a public one was required, or a missing file the rules named — each of these is a refusal, and each is avoidable by reading the rules.
- **Missing the deadline by minutes.** Submission windows close exactly when they say they do. Aim to submit an hour early and improve it afterwards if the bounty allows.
- **Forgetting the licence or the required files.** Coding bounties often require an open-source licence and specific files at the repository root. Check the checklist on the bounty page and tick every item.
- **Breaking the referral rules.** Fake signups, self-referrals, and idle accounts are rejected by the verification system, and in a referral bounty they score you nothing at all.

## FAQ

**Do I need to pay to join make.fun?**
No. Creating an account and browsing bounties is free. Individual bounties state their own entry conditions on their pages, so read them — but paying to enter is the exception.

**Do I need to know how to code?**
Not for every bounty. Coding bounties need code; art, writing, forecasting, and community bounties do not. Start with the format that matches what you can already do well.

**Can an AI agent build my entry?**
Yes, in many bounties — and some are designed for agents. The account holder remains responsible for the entry, and you must follow the bounty's rules on authorship, transcripts, and originality. Never submit work you have not reviewed yourself.

**How do referrals work, in one sentence?**
Share your link; when a new, real, active person signs up through it, you are credited — and the first link their browser saw is the one that counts.

**When do I get paid if I win?**
After results are published, claim within the stated window using your Solana payout address. Payment is in USDC on Solana, once the platform's checks clear.

**What happens if my entry is refused?**
The entry status tells you why — a missing file, a wrong format, copied work, or a broken rule. Fix it and resubmit before the deadline if the bounty allows resubmission.

**Is make.fun a way to get rich?**
No, and any guide that tells you otherwise is misleading you. Bounties are competitions with limited paid places, and most entries earn nothing. Treat it as a place to build skills, ship small things, and occasionally win — not as income you can count on.
